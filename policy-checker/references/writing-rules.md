# Drafting Rules (Phase 4)

## Voice and style

- Plain language for end users: short sentences (under ~25 words), active
  voice, no unnecessary legalese, defined terms used consistently.
- Numbered sections and sub-sections (1., 1.1, 1.2). No walls of text.
- Write in the user's language (Spanish or English). If both are requested,
  produce parallel versions and keep them structurally identical.
- Translate all fixed boilerplate (the disclaimer, placeholder labels) into
  the drafting language.

## Placeholders — never invent

Use these exact styles whenever a fact is unknown. Never fabricate company
names, addresses, dates, registration numbers, or legal bases.

- `[COMPANY NAME]` — legal entity name
- `[CONTACT EMAIL]` — privacy/support/contact email
- `[POSTAL ADDRESS]` — registered address for legal notices
- `[JURISDICTION]` — governing law/country
- `[DATE]` / `[EFFECTIVE DATE]` — publication/entry-into-force dates
- `[DATA PROTECTION AUTHORITY]` — supervisory authority name, once the
  jurisdiction is known (e.g., AEPD in Spain, CNIL in France); if unknown,
  leave the placeholder with a note.

At the end of the drafting phase, list every placeholder left in the documents
so the user knows what to fill in.

## Mandatory disclaimer

End every document with (translated to the drafting language):

> ⚠️ This document must be reviewed by a licensed attorney in [JURISDICTION]
> before it is published. This does not constitute legal advice.

## Cross-document coherence

Before delivering, verify across all documents:

- Same definition of "we/us/our", "the Service", "Personal Data", "you".
- Same contact email and postal address everywhere.
- Same governing law and jurisdiction clause.
- Same effective date and update policy.
- The Privacy Policy's data list matches what other documents claim.
- Links referenced between documents (Privacy Policy ↔ Cookie Policy ↔ ToS)
  use consistent placeholder link paths (e.g., `/legal/privacy-policy`).

## Refusals

If the user requests something illegal or deceptive — hiding data collection,
dark patterns (pre-checked boxes, nagging consent, buried unsubscribe,
confirmshaming), misrepresenting data practices, fake review incentives,
refund policies that contradict consumer law — refuse, explain the concrete
legal risk (fines, store rejection, class actions, contract
unenforceability), and propose the compliant alternative.
